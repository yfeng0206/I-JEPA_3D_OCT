## 1. Executive summary: 10 highest-value, concretely actionable rules for this paper

Ranked by expected value per unit of editing effort.

1. **Cap em dashes at 4 in the 9-page body; target 0 to 2.** `[E]` My measurement of 26 recent (Aug 2026) arXiv cs.CV self-supervised-learning papers: median 0.00 em dashes per 1,000 words, 57.7% contain **zero**, 90th percentile 0.69/1kw, maximum 2.18/1kw. My measurement of 15 landmark SSL/DL papers (I-JEPA, MAE, DINO, data2vec, DINOv2, SimCLR, BYOL, VICReg, Transformer, ViT, LoRA, Chinchilla, V-JEPA, MoCo, BatchNorm): median 0.00/1kw, 9 of 15 contain **zero**. A 9-page NeurIPS body is roughly 5,000 to 6,000 words, so the field's 90th percentile is about **4 em dashes for the whole body**. Details and the "is 66 anomalous" verdict in §2.

2. **Cut semicolon density by about 3x.** `[E]` This is a bigger outlier in your source than the em dash. Measured on `paper/genai4health2026/main_submission.tex`: 156 semicolons in 14,523 words = **10.7/1kw**. Measured on `main.tex`: 31 in 4,279 words = **7.2/1kw**. Same method on the 15 landmark papers: median **1.74/1kw** (I-JEPA 2.85, MAE 0.95, DINO 0.35, Transformer 0.00, ViT 5.15 as the high outlier). You are running 4x to 6x the field median. Semicolons are the classic "I stapled two sentences together" move and read as generated cadence. Replace most with a full stop.

3. **Purge the Kobak excess-vocabulary set, prioritising the 10 highest-value common words.** `[E]` Kobak et al. (Science Advances 2025, 15.1M PubMed abstracts) identified `across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within` as the single most diagnostic common-word set, with a combined frequency gap of 13.4 percentage points. Plus the rare-word markers `delve*, underscore*, showcase*, intricate/intricacies, realm, pivotal, leveraging, meticulous, seamless, multifaceted, groundbreaking, transformative, harness*, foundational, interplay, nuanced, noteworthy`. Full evidenced list in §3.

4. **Do not add an LLM-use disclosure statement.** `[E]` NeurIPS 2026 and NeurIPS 2025 both state explicitly that use of LLMs for writing, editing, formatting, grammar or basic code assistance **does not need to be documented**. Disclosure is required only if an LLM is "an important, original, or non-standard component of the approach." Adding an unnecessary disclosure in a double-blind submission buys you nothing and invites the AI-label credibility penalty (§4, and the disclosure-penalty literature in §3.5). Full verbatim policy in §4.

5. **Verify every citation and every number by hand before submission.** `[E]` This is the *only* LLM-related behaviour NeurIPS explicitly names as a sanctionable violation: "using references generated by an LLM without conducting the due diligence to verify correctness, existence and appropriateness in the manuscript's context." Penalty: revocation of publication status, at any time, including post-acceptance. This is a materially larger risk than style.

6. **Frame the null result as a measurement claim about the field, not a failure.** `[E]` Every high-impact ML null-result paper does this. Musgrave et al.: "we take a closer look at the field to see if this is actually true ... the actual improvements over time have been marginal at best." Narang et al.: "Surprisingly, we find that most modifications do not meaningfully improve performance." Full wording patterns and quotes in §6. NeurIPS 2026 even created a formal `Negative Results` contribution type for the main track.

7. **Quantify the null and state the minimum detectable effect.** `[E]` The NeurIPS checklist item on statistical significance (verbatim in §5.4) requires you to state what the error bars capture, how they were computed (bootstrap is explicitly named as an acceptable answer), the assumptions, and whether asymmetric intervals are being shown symmetrically. A calibration paper that reports "our design could have detected a difference of X AUC at 80% power; the observed difference was Y with 95% CI [a, b]" converts a null into a bound, which is a positive claim.

8. **Avoid the word "significant" almost entirely.** `[E]` In my sample of 40 recent SSL abstracts, only **3 of 40 (7.5%)** contained any form of "significan*". Reserve it for the strict statistical sense, or drop it. Never use it as an intensifier ("a significant improvement") when you mean "large".

9. **Split the method figure from the masking-policy figure, following I-JEPA exactly.** `[E]` I-JEPA has a minimal architecture figure (Fig. 3, `assets/ijepa.pdf`) with **6 short lower-case text labels plus 3 math symbols plus "L2"**, and a completely separate figure (Fig. 4) showing real images with the actual sampled masks and the exact scale/aspect hyperparameters in the caption. With six masking policies to compare, that separation is the strongest available template. Full anatomy in §7.

10. **Bold run-in lead-ins are fine; bolded phrases mid-sentence are not.** `[E]` I-JEPA, MAE and DINO all use bold run-in caption titles (`{\bf I-JEPA.}`, `\textbf{Our MAE architecture}.`, `\textbf{Effect of Patch Size.}`) and bold paragraph lead-ins. That is a genuine, long-standing ML convention and is *not* an AI tell. What is an AI tell is markdown-style boldface scattered inside sentences to emphasise key terms. Keep the former, remove the latter.

## 2. The em dash verdict

### 2.1 Is the em dash a real statistical marker of LLM text?

**Yes at the population level; no at the per-paper level. This distinction is the whole story.** `[E]`

The strongest source is a **pre-registered** study (OSF registration HFT8C) on the full medRxiv full-text XML corpus:

> "The prevalence of the em-dash in Discussion sections rose from 4.23% before the public release of ChatGPT to 11.58% afterward. This corresponds to an absolute increase of 7.35 percentage points (95% CI 6.94-7.77; odds ratio 2.96, 95% CI 2.77-3.17). The increase was not a sharp jump at the moment of ChatGPT's release, but rather a gradual, delayed acceleration: prevalence remained near 4% through 2023, reached 8.0% in 2024, and 20.3% in 2025. The effect survived every feasible sensitivity analysis (7.35-7.60 pp) and both falsification tests. A placebo temporal split within the pre-LLM era showed no meaningful change (+0.13 pp, 95% CI -0.33 to +0.58). In utility and boilerplate sections such as Acknowledgments and Data Availability, the effect was essentially absent."
>
> The authors are explicit about the limit: "The marker is a population-level indicator, not a per-paper detector of LLM use: it cannot establish whether a given manuscript was produced with artificial intelligence, and the study design cannot establish causality."

Source: *Em-ergence of the em-dash: a population-level rise in em-dash frequency in medRxiv preprints at the dawn of the large-language-model era*, arXiv:2606.29540v1 (28 Jun 2026), N = 69,632 first-version preprints 2020-2025. Methodologically clean: they used JATS XML specifically because it preserves the literal codepoint U+2014 and does not conflate it with U+002D, U+2010, U+2013 or U+2212.

**The number that matters for you is the base rate, not the increase.** Before ChatGPT, **95.8% of biomedical Discussion sections contained zero em dashes**. Even in 2025, at the peak, **79.7% contained zero**. A single em dash in an entire Discussion section is a minority event in human scientific prose.

Second source, on the model side: *The Last Fingerprint: How Markdown Training Shapes LLM Prose*, arXiv:2603.27006v1 (27 Mar 2026), E. M. Freeburg. Twelve models across five providers under a two-condition suppression experiment:

> "Em dash frequency and suppression resistance vary from 0.0 per 1,000 words (Llama) to 9.1 (GPT-4.1 under suppression), functioning as a signature of the specific fine-tuning procedure applied. A three-condition suppression gradient shows that even explicit em dash prohibition fails to eliminate the artifact in some models."

**Important counter-evidence that you should not ignore** `[E]`: the signal is drifting fast. Wikipedia's community-maintained `Signs of AI writing` page (which requires each claim to be corroborated by a reliable external source) records:

> "because LLMs' use of em-dashes has become somewhat notorious, some AI companies have attempted to make their newer chatbots suppress their use, most notably OpenAI's GPT-5.1. A July 2026 study found that of contemporary models only Claude used em dashes more than professional writers, and ChatGPT used them less." (citing Ars Technica, 14 Nov 2025, and The Economist, 30 Jul 2026)

So: as a *detector* the em dash is weakening in 2026. As a *reader-perception hazard* it is still live, because the folk belief among reviewers lags the model behaviour. And independently of the AI question, the accepted-paper base rate is near zero, so cutting them costs you nothing.

### 2.3 Is 66 in 9 pages anomalously high?

**Yes, by roughly an order of magnitude, if the count is of true em dashes (U+2014).** A 9-page NeurIPS body with figures is about 5,000 to 6,000 words. 66 / 5,500 x 1,000 = **~12 em dashes per 1,000 words**. That is:

- ~5.5x the maximum observed in my 26-paper 2026 sample (2.18/1kw)
- ~4.7x the maximum in my 15-paper landmark sample (Chinchilla, 2.53/1kw)
- **higher than GPT-4.1's rate under explicit markdown suppression (9.1/1kw)**, the highest model rate reported in the Freeburg study

**Caveat: I could not reproduce the count of 66 from the repository as it currently stands.** `[E]` Measured directly:

| Artifact | pages | em (U+2014) | en (U+2013) | words | em/1kw |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `paper/genai4health2026/main.pdf` | 12 | 5 | 30 | 5,201 | 0.96 |
| `paper/genai4health2026/main_workshop.pdf` | 4 | 6 | 7 | 1,349 | **4.45** |
| `paper/genai4health2026/main_submission.pdf` | 36 | 5 | 65 | 16,268 | 0.31 |

Source-level: all `.tex` under `paper/` total **30** `---` and **73** `--`. Many of the `--` are legitimate numeric ranges (`0.71--0.79`, `2020--2024`), which render as en dashes and are correct.

Three possible explanations for the discrepancy, in order of likelihood `[I]`:
1. The 66 count included en dashes and/or hyphens used as parenthetical breaks.
2. The 66 count is from a draft not committed to the repository.
3. The count came from the internal markdown notes rather than the manuscript. This is the one to worry about: `paper/genai4health2026/research/ablation_inventory.md` alone contains **452** em dashes; `autopilot/reports/P22_prose_edits.md` contains **153**; `autopilot/BACKGROUND_SIGNAL.md` contains **125**. If prose is being lifted from those notes into the paper, the rate will spike to exactly the anomalous level described above.

Note that `main_workshop.pdf` at **4.45 em/1kw already exceeds the maximum of my entire 26-paper 2026 sample**. So even the current committed build has a real problem in its short form.

### 2.4 What style guides say, and what to replace an em dash with

`[C]` Chicago Manual of Style treats the em dash as a strong break, stronger than a comma, less formal than parentheses. It is not "banned"; the issue is exclusively frequency. Chicago sets em dashes closed (no surrounding spaces); note that **spaced em dashes are themselves flagged as an AI tell** because LLM output typically spaces them while experienced human users do not (Wikipedia `WP:AIDASH`, citing Merrill and Kriss/NYT).

Replacement ladder, best first, for scientific prose `[C]`+`[I]`:

| Situation | Replace with |
|---|---|
| Parenthetical aside that is genuinely subordinate | **parentheses** `( )` |
| Parenthetical aside that is part of the argument | **commas** |
| Second clause explains or expands the first | **colon** |
| Second clause is a separate, closely related claim | **full stop.** New sentence. |
| Two independent clauses you want to bind | **full stop** (not a semicolon; see rule 2 above) |
| Numeric range, page range, year span, CI bounds | **en dash** (`--` in LaTeX). Keep these. |
| Sudden dramatic reversal | **restructure the sentence.** This is the case that produces the AI cadence. |

The last row is the important one. Most LLM em dashes are load-bearing for a rhetorical move ("the policy matched the baseline, and that is the point") rather than for syntax. Deleting the dash without deleting the move leaves the same cadence. Rewrite the sentence.

### 2.5 Recommended target for this paper

- **Body (9 pages): at most 4 em dashes. Ideal 0 to 2.** 4 corresponds to the field's 90th percentile (0.69/1kw x 5,500 words).
- **Zero in the abstract.** The abstract is the highest-scrutiny text in the paper and abstract-level excess-word analysis is exactly what the Kobak method operates on.
- **Zero in figure and table captions.**
- **Never two in the same paragraph.**
- **En dashes for ranges are unlimited and correct.** Do not "fix" `0.71--0.79`.
- **If you keep one, set it closed** (`word---word`, no spaces) per Chicago and to avoid the spaced-dash tell.

### 3.2 `[E-strong]` Multi-database full-text study, 2015-2024

*How much are LLMs changing the language of academic papers after ChatGPT? A multi-database and full text analysis*, arXiv:2509.09596. Six databases, plus 2.4M PMC open-access full texts.

> "Across databases, delve (+1,500%), underscore (+1,000%), and intricate (+700%) had the largest increases between 2022 and 2024. Growth in LLM-term usage was much higher in STEM fields than in social sciences and arts and humanities. In PMC full texts, the proportion of papers using **underscore six or more times** increased by over **10,000%** from 2022 to 2025, followed by intricate (+5,400%) and meticulous (+2,800%). ... in 2024, underscore strongly correlated with pivotal (0.449) and delve (0.311), compared with very weak associations in 2022 (0.032 and 0.018)."

**The co-occurrence finding is the practically important one.** A single "underscores" is noise. Three or four markers in the same paragraph is the actual signal. If you keep one or two of these words for genuine reasons, keep them far apart.

### 3.4 `[E-mod]` Reinhart et al.: syntactic, not just lexical, tells

*Do LLMs write like humans? Variation in grammatical and rhetorical styles*, arXiv:2410.16107 (PNAS 2025). Uses Biber's lexical/grammatical/rhetorical feature set on parallel human/LLM corpora.

> "the instruction-tuned LLMs used **present participial clauses at 2 to 5 times the rate of human text**, such as in this sentence from GPT-4o using two present participles: 'Bryan, leaning on his agility, dances around the ring, evading Show's heavy blows.' They also use **nominalizations at 1.5 to 2 times the rate of humans** ... On the other hand, GPT-4o uses the **agentless passive voice at roughly half the rate** as human texts."

> "The features that distinguish between humans and different LLMs include present participial clauses, 'that' clauses as sentence subject, passive voice, and nominalizations."

Word overuse ratios vs human rate: `camaraderie` 162x, `tapestry` 155x, `intricate` 119x, `underscore` 107x, `unspoken` 102x, `amidst` 100x. "'tapestry' appeared in 23% of GPT-4o outputs and 'amidst' in 27%."

**This gives you three concrete syntactic edits, which are more valuable than any word swap:**
1. **Kill trailing present-participial clauses.** The pattern "..., X, **enabling** Y", "..., **highlighting** Z", "..., **reflecting** W", "..., **suggesting** V". These are the "superficial analysis" appendage. In an ML paper the honest version is either a separate sentence with a claim you can defend, or nothing.
2. **De-nominalise.** "the utilisation of segmentation for the guidance of masking" -> "we use segmentation to guide masking".
3. **Do not over-correct the passive.** Human ML writing uses the agentless passive about twice as often as GPT-4o. Aggressive de-passivisation moves you further from the human distribution, not closer.

### 3.5 `[C]` Community-consensus tells with corroborating sources but no frequency measurement

From Wikipedia's `Signs of AI writing` (an unusually well-sourced community document; the vocabulary box explicitly requires "corroborated by at least one reliable, non-pop-science external source").

Curated `WP:AIVOCAB` list, verbatim:
> `Additionally` (especially beginning a sentence), `align with`, `boasts` (meaning "has"), `bolstered`, `crucial`, `deep dive`, `delve`, `emphasizing`, `enduring`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as a verb), `interplay`, `intricate/intricacies`, `key` (as an adjective), `landscape` (as an abstract noun), `meticulous/meticulously`, `pivotal`, `robust`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (as an abstract noun), `testament`, `underscore` (as a verb), `valuable`, `vibrant`

**Era-dependence, quoted, and this matters for a 2026 submission:**
> "The words that LLMs overuse have changed over time. For instance, the word delve was famously overused by ChatGPT in 2023 and early 2024, but became less frequent later in 2024, then dropped off sharply in 2025."
> - "2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): Additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant"
> - "Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant"
> - "**Mid-2025 and on (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing**"

So for a 2026 reader, the highest-salience words are `emphasizing`, `enhance`, `highlighting`, `showcasing`, not `delve`. Your existing list is slightly dated.

Other constructions documented there, each with citations to Russell, Kriss (NYT, 3 Dec 2025), and The Economist (30 Jul 2026), but **no measured frequency**:
- **Negative parallelisms**: "Not just X, but also Y", "Not X, but Y", "X rather than Y". `[C]`
- **Rule of three / tricolon**: "adjective, adjective, adjective" or "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase". Quoted: "LLMs often use this structure to make superficial analyses appear more comprehensive." `[C]`
- **Undue emphasis on significance / legacy / broader trends**: `stands/serves as`, `is a testament to`, `plays a crucial/pivotal/vital/key role`, `underscores/highlights its importance`, `reflects broader`, `contributing to the`, `setting the stage for`, `marks a shift`, `evolving landscape`, `focal point`. `[C]` **This is the single most dangerous family for your Discussion section**, because a null-result paper is under exactly the pressure that produces this puffery.
- **Superficial analyses via trailing participles**: `highlighting...`, `ensuring...`, `reflecting...`, `contributing to...`, `fostering...`, `encompassing...`, `enhancing...`, `valuable insights`, `align/resonate with`. `[C]`, and corroborated syntactically by Reinhart et al. `[E-mod]`
- **Overuse of boldface**: "emphasize every instance of a chosen word or phrase, often in a 'key takeaways' fashion". `[C]`
- **Inline-header vertical lists**: bold lead-in, colon, then a bullet. `[C]`
- **Curly quotation marks and apostrophes**, **spaced em dashes**, **title case headings**. `[C]`

### 5.2 Measured structural conventions

`[E]` My measurement on 40 recent arXiv cs.CV self-supervised papers (titles/abstracts) and 25 of those with parseable LaTeX:

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Titles containing a colon | 22 / 40 = **55%** |
| Abstract length (words) | median **195.5**, p10 = 160, p90 = 256, min 110, max 279 |
| Abstracts containing "significan*" | **3 / 40 = 7.5%** |
| Related Work as section 1 or 2 (i.e. early) | **11 / 25 = 44%** |
| Contributions as a bullet/enumerate list in the intro | **12 / 25 = 48%** |

Dominant section order, observed verbatim in the majority of parseable papers:
> `Introduction > Related Work > Method(ology) > Experiments (or Experimental Setup + Results) > Conclusion`

Variants seen: `Introduction > Method > Experimental Protocol > Results > Conclusion` (Related Work folded into the intro), and the medical-journal-influenced `Introduction > Methods > Results > Discussion > Conclusion`.

**Recommendations for a 9-page workshop paper** `[I]` grounded in the above:
- Related Work early or late is a genuine coin flip (44% early). For a **null-result** paper, put it **early**: the reader needs to know that segmenter-driven masking is an established claim before you report that it does not beat the segmentation-free policy. Otherwise the null has no antagonist.
- Contribution list: a 3-to-4-item bulleted list is used by roughly half the field and is safe. **Avoid exactly three bullets with parallel gerund openings** ("Introducing...", "Demonstrating...", "Releasing..."), which is the tricolon tell in its most concentrated form. Use 4 items, or 2, or vary the syntax.
- Abstract: aim for **180 to 230 words, one paragraph**, containing: the setting, the controlled comparison you ran, the number of policies and the evaluation, the headline null with its effect size and interval, and one sentence on what practitioners should do differently. Do not open with a "the landscape of X has been transformed by Y" sentence.

### 5.4 Reporting statistics: the checklist item, verbatim

`[E]` This is the authoritative statement of the convention for your venue family. From NeurIPS 2026 `checklist.tex`, item "Experiment statistical significance":

> "Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
>
> Guidelines:
> - The authors should answer Yes if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.
> - **The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated** (for example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given experimental conditions).
> - **The method for calculating the error bars should be explained** (closed form formula, call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
> - The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
> - It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of the mean.
> - It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis of Normality of errors is not verified.
> - **For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range** (e.g., negative error rates).
> - If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text."

**Concrete implications for an AUC + bootstrap + multiplicity-correction paper** `[I]` derived from the above:

1. State explicitly what your bootstrap resamples over. Patients? Eyes? B-scans? Seeds? Pretraining runs? This is the "factors of variability" requirement and it is the most common thing reviewers attack in medical-imaging SSL papers. If you resample test cases but not pretraining seeds, say so, because the CI then does not cover pretraining variance.
2. Report `n` at every level: n patients, n eyes, n volumes, n B-scans, n positive cases, n pretraining runs per policy, n bootstrap replicates, and the RNG seed.
3. Bootstrap percentile intervals are asymmetric. **Report them as `[lo, hi]`, never as `mean +/- x`.** The checklist calls this out explicitly.
4. For comparing two policies, report the **paired** difference and its CI (`ΔAUC = 0.004, 95% CI [-0.011, 0.019]`), not two overlapping marginal CIs. Non-overlap of marginal CIs is neither necessary nor sufficient for a difference. This is the single most common statistical error in AUC comparison papers. `[C]`
5. On the word "significant": use it **only** in the strict statistical sense, and prefer to avoid it entirely. Only 7.5% of my 40-abstract sample used any form of it. The ASA Statement on p-Values (Wasserstein & Lazar, *The American Statistician* 70(2), 2016) and Amrhein, Greenland & McShane (*Nature* 567, 2019, "Retire statistical significance") are the canonical citations if a reviewer pushes back on your avoiding dichotomised claims. `[C]`
6. Preferred phrasings for a null `[I]`:
   - "We did not detect a difference between X and Y (ΔAUC = 0.004, 95% CI [-0.011, 0.019])."
   - "The interval excludes differences larger than 0.019 AUC, i.e. our design rules out the effect sizes claimed in prior work."
   - "Consistent with no benefit at the resolution of this study."
   - **Avoid**: "there was no significant difference" (dichotomises), "the policies performed identically" (overclaims the null), "X was slightly better but not significantly" (the worst of both).
7. State the multiplicity correction method and the family over which it was applied, before showing the corrected numbers. Reviewers will otherwise assume you chose the family post hoc.

### 5.5 Figure caption conventions

`[E]` The NeurIPS 2026 template's own sample caption is itself the instruction, quoted verbatim:

> "Sample figure caption. **Explain what the figure shows and add a key take-away message to the caption.**"

`[E]` Measured against real accepted papers, the convention resolves into two caption genres:

- **Method/architecture figures get a walkthrough, no takeaway.** I-JEPA's method caption (Fig. 3, ~90 words, 5 sentences) defines every visual element in order and stops. It makes no claim.
- **Results figures get a takeaway.** I-JEPA's Fig. 1 caption ends: "By predicting in representation space, I-JEPA produces semantic representations while using less compute than previous methods."

Other measured properties of accepted captions:
- **Length: roughly 60 to 100 words, 3 to 6 sentences** for a main figure. I-JEPA method 90 words; MAE architecture 80 words; data2vec 60 words; I-JEPA masking figure 75 words.
- **Bold run-in title.** `{\bf I-JEPA.}`, `\textbf{Our MAE architecture}.`, `{\bf ImageNet Linear Evaluation}.`, `{\bf Scaling}.`, `\textbf{Effect of Patch Size.}`, `\textbf{Collapse study.}` This is universal in this literature.
- **Self-contained.** Every symbol in the figure is defined in the caption. I-JEPA's caption names the context encoder, the predictor, the positional tokens, the target encoder, and the EMA, because none of those are spelled out in the artwork itself.
- **Hyperparameters live in the caption when they are the point.** I-JEPA's masking figure caption: "we randomly sample 4 target blocks with scale in the range (0.15, 0.2) and aspect ratio in the range (0.75, 1.5). Next, we randomly sample a context block with scale in the range (0.85, 1.0) and remove any overlapping target blocks." That is the model for your six-policy figure.
- **Sub-panel references use bold letters in parentheses**: `{\bf(a)}`, `\textbf{(left)}`, `\textbf{(right)}`.
- Rougier, Droettboom & Bourne, "Ten Simple Rules for Better Figures", *PLoS Comput Biol* 10(9):e1003833, Rule 4: "Captions Are Not Optional ... The caption explains how to read the figure and provides additional precision for what cannot be graphically represented. This can be thought of as the explanation you would give during an oral presentation ... but with the difference that you must think in advance about the questions people would ask." `[E]` (peer-reviewed)

## 6. Null-result rhetoric: patterns that work, with real examples

All quotes below are verbatim from the abstracts of published, well-cited papers whose central finding is negative. These are the register to imitate.

### 6.1 The eight moves, with evidence

**Move 1: Make the claim about the field's evidence, not about your method's failure.**
> "Deep metric learning papers from the past four years have consistently claimed great advances in accuracy, often more than doubling the performance of decade-old methods. **In this paper, we take a closer look at the field to see if this is actually true.** We find flaws in the experimental methodology of numerous metric learning papers, and show that the actual improvements over time have been marginal at best."
> - Musgrave, Belongie & Lim, *A Metric Learning Reality Check*, ECCV 2020, arXiv:2003.08505

Note the construction: a claim attributed to the literature, then "we take a closer look", then the negative finding. The paper is never on the defensive because the burden was never on it.

**Move 2: Say "surprisingly" once, flatly, and move on.**
> "**Surprisingly, we find that most modifications do not meaningfully improve performance.** Furthermore, most of the Transformer variants we found beneficial were either developed in the same codebase that we used or are relatively minor changes."
> - Narang et al., *Do Transformer Modifications Transfer Across Implementations and Applications?*, EMNLP 2021, arXiv:2102.11972

**Move 3: Report the null as a *count*, which converts absence into a measurement.**
> "Specifically, we considered 18 algorithms that were presented at top-level research conferences in the last years. **Only 7 of them could be reproduced with reasonable effort. For these methods, it however turned out that 6 of them can often be outperformed with comparably simple heuristic methods** ... The remaining one clearly outperformed the baselines but did not consistently outperform a well-tuned non-neural linear ranking method."
> - Dacrema, Cremonesi & Jannach, *Are We Really Making Much Progress?*, RecSys 2019, arXiv:1907.06902

**Move 4: Identify the confound that explains the field's positive results. This is the actual contribution.**
> "we demonstrate the sensitivity of optimizer comparisons to the hyperparameter tuning protocol. **Our findings suggest that the hyperparameter search space may be the single most important factor explaining the rankings obtained by recent empirical comparisons in the literature.** In fact, we show that these results can be contradicted when hyperparameter search spaces are changed."
> - Choi et al., *On Empirical Comparisons of Optimizers for Deep Learning*, arXiv:1910.05446

For your paper, the analogue is: what property of the anatomy-guided masking literature made it look like segmentation helped? Coverage? Block scale? Compute-matching? Name it.

**Move 5: Ship a positive by-product, explicitly labelled.**
> "Our study shows that XGBoost outperforms these deep models across the datasets, including the datasets used in the papers that proposed the deep models. We also demonstrate that XGBoost requires much less tuning. **On the positive side, we show that an ensemble of deep models and XGBoost performs better on these datasets than XGBoost alone.**"
> - Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, *Tabular Data: Deep Learning is Not All You Need*, arXiv:2106.03253

The phrase "On the positive side," is a small, cheap, human-sounding hinge. Your positive by-product is obvious and strong: **the segmentation-free policy matches, so you can drop the segmenter.** That is a cost, dependency, and failure-mode reduction. Lead the abstract's final sentence with it.

**Move 6: A simple baseline "surprisingly" matching is a contribution, not an embarrassment.**
> "**a modified baseline method that surprisingly achieves competitive performance** when compared with the state-of-the-art"
> - Chen et al., *A Closer Look at Few-shot Classification*, ICLR 2019, arXiv:1904.04232

**Move 7: State the boundary condition explicitly, both directions.**
> "**pre-training benefits when we use one fifth of the labeled data, and hurts accuracy when we use all labeled data.** ... In other words, self-training works well exactly on the same setup that pre-training does not work."
> - Zoph et al., *Rethinking Pre-training and Self-training*, NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2006.06882

A null with a stated regime of validity is a claim. A null without one is a shrug.

**Move 8: Convert the null into a recommendation.**
> "We conjecture that performance improvements may strongly depend on implementation details and correspondingly **make some recommendations for improving the generality of experimental results.**" (Narang et al.)
> "This study leads us to **propose recommendations for performance comparisons.**"
> - Bouthillier et al., *Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks*, MLSys 2021, arXiv:2103.03098
> "We conclude with **a set of best practices for reporting experimental results** which allow for robust future comparisons."
> - Dodge et al., *Show Your Work*, EMNLP 2019, arXiv:1909.03004

### 6.2 Title patterns for null-result papers `[E]`

Observed in the corpus above:
- Direct question: *"Are We Really Making Much Progress?"*, *"Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?"*, *"Do Transformer Modifications Transfer Across Implementations and Applications?"*
- Re-examination: *"A Metric Learning Reality Check"*, *"A Closer Look at Few-shot Classification"*, *"Rethinking Pre-training and Self-training"*
- Bounded negative claim: *"Tabular Data: Deep Learning is Not All You Need"*

`[I]` For yours, the strongest frames are the question form ("Does anatomy-guided masking help I-JEPA on retinal OCT?") or the bounded-claim form ("Anatomy-guided masking does not outperform a segmentation-free policy for I-JEPA on retinal OCT"). The bounded form is more honest and reads less like clickbait; the question form is more common in the corpus.

### 6.3 What to avoid, specifically for a null-result paper

`[I]`, grounded in §3.5's "undue emphasis on significance" tell:

- Do not pre-empt objections you have not been given. Deleting three defensive sentences from the Discussion usually raises the paper's confidence more than adding an experiment.
- Do not write "while our results are negative, they nonetheless..." That construction, and its relatives ("this does not diminish...", "importantly, this should not be interpreted as..."), is the defensive register. State the finding, state its scope, stop.
- Do not inflate the null into a claim about "the broader landscape of self-supervised learning" or "important implications for the field". This is exactly the AI-puffery pattern and it also weakens the paper. Your scope is: one architecture, one modality, one dataset, one downstream task, six policies.
- Do not hedge every sentence. Hedge once, precisely, in the Limitations section, with numbers.
- Do use the phrase "we did not detect" rather than "there is no". You measured a bound, not an absence.

### 7.1 I-JEPA Figure 3 (the method figure), described concretely

2. **Far left, bottom:** a thumbnail labelled `target`. Same image, un-erased, with **three coloured rectangles (blue, red, yellow)** drawn on it marking the three target blocks.
3. **Second column:** two tall, narrow, pale-grey rounded rectangles, one per thumbnail. Each contains **only a math symbol**: `f_theta` (top) and `f_theta-bar` (bottom). The words `context encoder` and `target encoder` sit **above** each box in small lower-case type, on two lines.
4. **From the top encoder:** a small 2x2 grey patch grid, then **the only solid arrow in the figure**, pointing right.
5. **Middle, three parallel rows** (one per target block). Each row is: a small patch grid containing grey tokens (context) plus a coloured block of positional mask tokens in the matching colour, then a pale-grey rounded box containing `g_phi`, then the predicted coloured patch. The word `predictor` is centred **above** the column of three `g_phi` boxes, so one label serves three instances.
6. **From the bottom encoder:** a grey patch grid with the three coloured rectangles drawn on it, with three short grey arrows pointing **leftward into it** (indicating extraction of the three target representations).
7. **Right margin:** **dashed dark-blue lines** run from each of the three predicted patches and each of the three target patches, converging at `L2`.

**How the EMA target encoder is drawn: it is not drawn.** There is no dashed feedback arrow, no "EMA" box, no stop-gradient symbol. The entire mechanism is carried by (a) the bar over theta in `f_theta-bar` and (b) one sentence of caption. Verbatim caption (label `fig:method`):

> "**I-JEPA.** The Image-based Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture uses a single context block to predict the representations of various target blocks originating from the same image. The context encoder is a Vision Transformer (ViT), which only processes the visible context patches. The predictor is a narrow ViT that takes the context encoder output and, conditioned on positional tokens (shown in color), predicts the representations of a target block at a specific location. The target representations correspond to the outputs of the target-encoder, the weights of which are updated at each iteration via an exponential moving average of the context encoder weights."

**Colour semantics:** colour encodes **block identity only** (blue / red / yellow = target block 1 / 2 / 3). Grey = "generic token, not the thing being tracked". Three colours plus grey. No gradient, no colormap, no decorative colour.

**Line semantics:** solid arrow = forward computation; dashed line = loss connection; short grey arrow = indexing/extraction. Three line types, each used consistently.

**Crucially, masking gets its own separate figure.** I-JEPA Figure 4 (`assets/mask-samples-4.pdf`, 982 x 697 pt, wide, columns labelled `original` / `context` / `targets`) shows four real images with the actual sampled masks, and the caption carries the exact hyperparameters:

> "Examples of our context and target-masking strategy. Given an image, we randomly sample 4 target blocks with scale in the range (0.15, 0.2) and aspect ratio in the range (0.75, 1.5). Next, we randomly sample a context block with scale in the range (0.85, 1.0) and remove any overlapping target blocks. Under this strategy, the target-blocks are relatively semantic, and the context-block is informative, yet sparse (efficient to process)."

- **Total text in the figure: four words.** `input`, `encoder`, `decoder`, `target`. Plus two vertical `...` ellipses.
- Flow, strictly left to right, no crossings, no dashed lines, no feedback:
  `input` (5x5 patch grid, masked patches are flat grey) -> arrow -> a vertical column of **only the visible patches** (visually enacting the claim that the encoder never sees mask tokens) -> grey rounded box `encoder` -> column of cyan tokens -> arrow -> column of **interleaved cyan and grey** tokens (mask tokens inserted after the encoder) -> grey rounded box `decoder` -> column of salmon tokens -> arrow -> `target` (full image grid).
- **The geometry carries the paper's main claim.** The `encoder` box is drawn visibly taller and larger than the `decoder` box, because asymmetric encoder/decoder capacity is the contribution. A reader who only looks at the picture still learns the thesis.
- Colour semantics: cyan = encoded visible tokens, grey = mask tokens, salmon = decoder outputs. Three colours, each meaning one thing.

Caption verbatim:
> "**Our MAE architecture**. During pre-training, a large random subset of image patches (e.g., 75%) is masked out. The encoder is applied to the small subset of *visible patches*. Mask tokens are introduced *after* the encoder, and the full set of encoded patches and mask tokens is processed by a small decoder that reconstructs the original image in pixels. After pre-training, the decoder is discarded and the encoder is applied to uncorrupted images (full sets of patches) for recognition tasks."

Note the italics doing load-bearing work on the two words that matter (`visible patches`, `after`).

### 7.3 data2vec Figure 1

`[E]` Caption verbatim (arXiv:2202.03555, `illustration.pdf`):
> "Illustration of how data2vec follows the same learning process for different modalities. The model first produces representations of the original input example (teacher mode) which are then regressed by the same model based on a masked version of the input. The **teacher parameters are an exponentially moving average of the student weights**. The student predicts the average of K network layers of the teacher (**shaded in blue**)."

Same pattern: EMA stated in words, not drawn. Colour (blue shading) used to mark exactly one thing (which layers are averaged).

### 7.4 The extractable template

`[I]`, derived from the three exemplars above:

1. **Two figures, not one.** Architecture figure (abstract, symbols, no data) + policy/masking figure (real OCT B-scans, real masks, hyperparameters in the caption). With six masking policies this is not optional.
2. **Text budget for the architecture figure: under 12 words plus math symbols.** Everything else goes in the caption.
3. **Label boxes with math symbols inside and words above.** `f_theta` inside, "context encoder" above.
4. **One solid arrow direction. Dashed lines reserved for the loss.** No bidirectional arrows, no crossings.
5. **Never draw the EMA.** Write it. All three exemplars do this.
6. **Depict masking on a real image, by erasure or by coloured overlay.** Not as an abstract grid of grey squares in the architecture figure.
7. **Colour encodes identity, never decoration.** Three or four colours maximum, plus grey for "not tracked". For a six-policy figure, use one panel per policy on the same B-scan rather than six colours in one panel.
8. **Let geometry carry the claim.** MAE draws a big encoder and a small decoder. If your six policies differ in coverage fraction, make the visible area of the overlays proportional and true to the actual numbers.
9. **Sizing:** single-column-width, portrait, for an architecture figure with vertical branching; full-width, landscape, for a strict pipeline.

### 7.5 Common failure modes

Grounded where possible, labelled honestly. I searched for a systematic study of reviewer complaints about method figures and **did not find one**; the general-figure literature is the best available evidence.

`[E]` (Rougier, Droettboom & Bourne 2014, peer-reviewed, PLoS Comput Biol):
- **Rule 5, "Do Not Trust the Defaults":** "these settings are to be used for virtually any type of plot, they are not fine-tuned for a specific type of plot. In other words, they are good enough for any plot but they are best for none." Default matplotlib/draw.io output is a recognisable tell of a figure nobody spent time on.
- **Rule 6, "Use Color Effectively":** "Do not use the default colormap (e.g., jet or rainbow) unless there is an explicit reason to do so ... avoid using too many similar colors since color blindness may make it difficult to discern some color differences."
- **Rule 7, "Do Not Mislead the Reader":** the truncated-y-axis and area-vs-radius examples. Directly relevant if you plot six AUCs that differ by 0.01 on a y-axis running 0.78 to 0.82.
- **Rule 3:** a journal figure can afford detail because "the reader is able to view the figure as long as necessary"; a slide figure cannot. Do not reuse a talk figure in the paper.

`[E]` NeurIPS 2026 template: "All artwork must be neat, clean, and legible. Lines should be dark enough for reproduction purposes. ... You may use color figures. However, it is best for the figure captions and the paper body to be legible if the paper is printed in either black/white or in color."

`[I]` Failure modes I infer from the contrast between the exemplars above and typical submissions:
- **Text overload.** I-JEPA's method figure has 6 label phrases. A figure with 40 words of annotation is doing the caption's job badly.
- **Every arrow the same.** If forward pass, EMA update, gradient stop, and loss all use identical black arrows, the reader cannot recover the algorithm. Use exactly three visually distinct line types and define them.
- **Drawing the EMA.** Adds a feedback arrow that visually implies a cycle, which is wrong (it is not backpropagated). All three exemplars avoid it.
- **Screenshotted architecture diagrams.** Blurry text at 200% zoom is a visible signal of low effort.
- **Font size below the body text size.** If the figure's smallest label is smaller than a footnote, it is illegible in print.
- **Colour used decoratively**, so the reader tries to decode a meaning that is not there.
- **The six-policy problem specifically:** six policies in one panel with six colours will be unreadable. Use a 2x3 or 1x6 grid of small multiples on the same B-scan, with the policy name above each panel and a shared caption.

### 7.6 Vector vs raster

`[E]` **Direct evidence from the artifacts:** I-JEPA's `assets/ijepa.pdf` and MAE's `fig/arch.pdf` are both vector PDFs containing live, extractable text (I read out the label strings and their coordinates). Raster content is confined to the photo thumbnails and patch textures, which genuinely are photographs. This is the actual practice of the two most-cited papers in your exact subfield.

`[C]` NeurIPS's own instruction is weaker and format-agnostic: "All artwork must be neat, clean, and legible. Lines should be dark enough for reproduction purposes." It does not mandate vector.

`[C]` CVPR/ICCV author-kit guidance (secondary sources; I did not fetch the primary author kit) recommends vector PDF/EPS for diagrams and plots and high-resolution raster (>= 300 dpi) only for photographs, with fonts embedded.

`[I]` Practical rule for your paper: **every plot and every diagram as PDF; only the OCT B-scans as raster, at native resolution or higher.** Your `figures/` directory is already all-PDF, which is correct. Verify at camera-ready that fonts are embedded and that no PDF figure contains an embedded low-resolution bitmap of a plot. Also check that figure text renders at a size comparable to the body text after `\includegraphics` scaling; the most common defect is a matplotlib figure exported at default size then scaled to `0.45\linewidth`, which halves the effective font size.

## 9. Gaps and uncertainties

Stated plainly.

1. **I could not verify the "66 em dashes" figure.** The repository's current build has 5 to 6 em dashes per compiled PDF. See §2.3 for the three candidate explanations and why the third (prose migrating in from the markdown research notes, which contain 452 / 153 / 127 / 125 em dashes in individual files) is the one worth acting on.
2. **I could not access OpenReview.** Both the HTML group page and `api2.openreview.net` are behind a browser challenge. I therefore have **no direct measurement of accepted GenAI4Health 2025 workshop papers** (abstract length, structure, figure count). My structural numbers in §5.2 come from recent arXiv cs.CV SSL papers, which is a reasonable but not identical population. Suggested follow-up: open https://openreview.net/group?id=NeurIPS.cc/2025/Workshop/GenAI4Health in a browser and sample 10 accepted Track-1 papers.
3. **No study exists on ML peer reviewers penalising AI-sounding prose.** I searched for it directly. The disclosure-penalty literature (§3.8) is on news and creative writing, not peer review. Do not let anyone tell you this transfer is established.
4. **The em-dash signal is weakening in 2026.** The Economist's July 2026 measurement (via Wikipedia's citation) found ChatGPT now uses em dashes *less* than professional writers, and GPT-5.1 explicitly suppresses them. My recommendation in §2.5 therefore rests more on the accepted-paper base rate (near zero, robustly measured) than on the detector argument.
5. **"moreover", "furthermore", "it is important to note", excessive hedging, and uniform paragraph lengths have no empirical support that I could locate.** They may still be worth editing on ordinary style grounds, but not on evidence grounds. See §3.6.
6. **My word counts are approximate.** The de-LaTeXing regex strips macros and math crudely, and includes appendix text. Em-dash and semicolon *counts* are exact; the per-1,000-word *rates* carry maybe +/- 15% error. The comparison across papers is apples-to-apples because the same method was applied throughout.
7. **Semicolon counts include math-mode semicolons.** For papers with heavy notation this inflates the count. It inflates your paper's count too, so the 4x-to-6x ratio in rule 2 is likely a slight overstatement. Recount on rendered text before acting if you want precision.
8. **The GenAI4Health 2026 call was not available.** I used the 2025 call as the spec. Verify the 2026 page limit, template version, and track structure before submission; the 2025 edition allowed 9 pages for Track 1, which matches your paper.
9. **I did not fetch the ACL policy or the CVPR author kit primary sources.** Both are cited from secondary summaries and are marked as such.
